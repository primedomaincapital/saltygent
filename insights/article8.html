The Grooming Budget: What a Well-Kept Man Actually Spends in a Year

GROOMING · 2026 · 7 min read

The receipt is the easiest way to understand the grooming budget, because the numbers are small enough to ignore and large enough to matter. A haircut here, a bottle of shampoo there, a fragrance at Christmas, a razor subscription that renews before anyone remembers signing up. None of it feels like spending. Added up over a year, it is one of the largest discretionary lines in a man's budget — and one of the least examined, because nobody totals it. The average American household spent $978 on personal care products and services in 2024, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Expenditure Survey, up from $950 in 2023 and $866 in 2022. That figure covers the whole household, not one man, but it sets the scale: grooming is a four-figure annual habit hiding in two-figure transactions.

The largest single line is the haircut, and its price depends entirely on which chair the man sits in. The tiers in 2026 are cleanly separated: a basic cut runs $18 to $30 at a budget chain, $35 to $55 at a standard shop, and $55 to $95 at a premium provider. SQUIRE's 2026 barbershop report puts the average cut at $43, and says the average client gets about seven haircuts a year — $301 a year before tips, or $361 with a 20 percent tip. A 2025 Talker Research poll of 2,000 American men for Sport Clips found the average man gets ten haircuts a year, which at $43 is $430 before tips and $516 with them. The gap between seven and ten cuts is the gap between a man who stretches the interval and a man who keeps the shape, and it is worth $115 to $155 a year.

The full visit costs more than the cut. A beard trim runs $10 to $18 at a budget chain, $15 to $25 at a standard shop, and $25 to $40 at a premium provider; a full beard shaping with scissors, razor, and hot towel runs $20 to $30, $30 to $45, and $45 to $65 across the same tiers. A hot towel shave is $25 to $35, $35 to $50, and $50 to $75. The bundle — cut plus beard — is $30 to $45, $50 to $75, and $80 to $120. A standard shop visit with cut and beard shaping runs about $89 out the door with an 18 percent tip; a premium visit with cut, beard, and product runs about $171. At nine visits a year, the budget tier costs about $270, the standard tier about $801, and the premium tier about $1,539. At thirteen visits, the premium tier reaches $2,223. The chair is the budget.

What the products actually cost

The products are the quiet half of the ledger. The Bureau of Labor Statistics splits the household's $978 into $499 on products and $479 on services, and within products the largest published line is cosmetics, perfume, and bath preparations at $257 per household in 2024. A 2025 market analysis from Statista found the average man in North America spends over $300 a year on shaving supplies and the skincare to fix the damage — the multi-blade razors, the shaving creams, the pre-shave oils, the aftershaves, and the balms and serums meant to calm the face down afterward. The number is a reminder that shaving is not one purchase but a system, and the system is priced to renew.

Fragrance is the most concentrated spend in the routine. A 100-millilitre bottle of a good eau de toilette runs $100 to $200, and at two or three sprays a day it lasts six to twelve months — which makes it $100 to $200 a year for the man who wears one scent, and double that for the man who rotates two. It is also the spend with the highest visible return per dollar, because scent is the only part of the routine other people register without looking. Skincare is the opposite: the spend with the lowest visible return per dollar at the top end. Only 39 percent of American men say they have a consistent skincare routine, and the difference between a $12 cleanser and a $120 serum is visible to no one but the man who bought it.

The rotation is where the fragrance budget either holds or escapes. One scent, worn daily, is the cheapest and the most distinctive choice — it becomes the man's signature, and the bottle lasts the better part of a year. Two scents, one for day and one for evening, double the spend and halve the life of each bottle, which is a fair trade for the man who wants the option. Three or more, and the bottles expire before they are finished: most fragrances keep for three to five years once opened, and a collection of half-used bottles is the grooming equivalent of a closet full of unworn shirts. The discipline is the same as the wardrobe — buy the one, wear it well, replace it when it is gone — and the men who follow it spend $100 to $200 a year on scent. The men who do not spend twice that and smell of nothing in particular.

The at-home question is the last line in the budget, and it is the one most men get wrong in the other direction. A home setup — a good clipper, a trimmer, a razor, and the products to maintain them — costs $100 to $200 once, and it tempts the man to cut his own hair. The arithmetic looks persuasive until the result is considered: a home cut rarely matches a barber's blend and detailing, and the money saved on the chair is spent on the correction. The at-home kit earns its place on the maintenance work — the neckline between cuts, the beard line, the daily shave — and the professional keeps the work that shows. The grooming budget is not a contest between home and shop. It is a division of labor, and the labor that is visible belongs to the professional.

The counterargument: the price keeps rising, and the time does not

There is a case that grooming has become a tax on looking employed, and the price data supports it. The Bureau of Labor Statistics index for haircuts and other personal care services rose 37.6 percent from the 2019 annual average to July 2026, while the all-items consumer price index rose 30.6 percent over the same period. Haircuts got more expensive faster than almost everything else, and the gap is widening. At the same time, the time men spend grooming has barely moved: the American Time Use Survey puts the average at 0.58 hours a day in 2025, about 35 minutes, against 0.55 hours in 2003. Men are paying substantially more for the same thirty-five minutes, which is the definition of a price increase rather than a lifestyle change.

The honest budget, then, is three tiers with a clear rule for each. The drugstore tier — a $25 chain cut every five weeks, drugstore shampoo and shaving supplies, one fragrance a year — runs roughly $500 to $700 a year, and it is perfectly adequate. The barbershop tier — a $43 cut every five weeks with a tip, a proper beard trim, mid-range skincare and one good fragrance — runs roughly $900 to $1,200, and it is where the visible difference lives: the shape of the cut, the condition of the skin, the scent. The premium tier — a $75 cut with beard work and product, a skincare routine with serums, two fragrances — runs $1,500 to $2,200, and the extra money buys pleasure and ritual more than appearance. The upgrades worth paying for are the ones other people can see: the cut, the clean shave, the scent. The upgrades worth skipping are the ones only the receipt can see.
