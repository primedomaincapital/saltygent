The Watch: Why Timepieces Still Define the Modern Gentleman

WHAT DOES DRESSING WELL MEAN  ·  4 min read

In 2015, the obituaries for the mechanical watch were being written. The Apple Watch had just launched, selling an estimated 12 million units in its first year. Swiss watch exports were declining. Pundits declared that a generation raised on smartphones would never strap a tiny machine to their wrists just to tell time — a function their phone performed better, for free.

A decade later, the Swiss watch industry is having its best run in history. Exports hit a record 26.7 billion Swiss francs in 2023, according to the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry. The secondary market for luxury timepieces — led by Rolex, Patek Philippe, and Audemars Piguet — saw prices for coveted models trade at two to three times retail during the 2021-2022 peak. Even after the correction, demand for mechanical watches among buyers under 40 has never been higher. The generation that was supposed to kill the watch became its most enthusiastic customer.

The lesson isn’t about horology. It’s about what objects mean. In an age when everything is digital, disposable, and upgradeable, a mechanical watch is none of those things. That’s exactly the point.

What a watch communicates

Clothing sends signals, but they’re noisy. A suit can be rented. Shoes can be faked. A watch, worn daily for years, becomes something else: a statement of values made through the quiet accumulation of mornings.

The watch world understands this implicitly, which is why it segments so precisely by personality. The Rolex Submariner says achievement — the wearer has arrived, and wants you to know it without having to say it. The Omega Speedmaster says taste — the wearer knows the Moonwatch story, appreciates the history, and chose the insider’s pick over the obvious one. The Grand Seiko says discernment — the wearer looked past Swiss marketing and found Japanese finishing that rivals anything from Geneva at half the price. The Casio F-91W, worn ironically or not by everyone from tech billionaires to college students, says confidence — the wearer doesn’t need your approval.

This is why watch advice that focuses on “investment value” misses the point so completely. Yes, certain Rolex and Patek Philippe models have appreciated. But buying a watch as an investment is like buying a painting as insulation — it misunderstands the object’s purpose. A watch is the most personal object a man owns. It’s chosen, worn daily, and seen by everyone he meets. The question isn’t “will it hold value?” It’s “does it say what I mean?”

The mechanical miracle

There’s a technical argument for mechanical watches that rarely gets made clearly. A modern automatic movement contains 200 to 300 individual components, many smaller than a grain of rice, assembled by hand, operating with tolerances measured in microns. It runs without batteries, without charging, without software updates. It will run for a century with periodic servicing. It is, by any reasonable measure, a miracle of miniaturization that we take for granted because it sits on wrists instead of in museums.

Compare this to a smartwatch. The Apple Watch Ultra — an excellent device — will be obsolete in five years. Its battery degrades. Its software stops updating. It becomes e-waste with a fond memory. A mechanical watch from 1970, serviced properly, runs as well today as the day it was made. One is a product. The other is an heirloom.

This durability is what the younger generation of collectors intuited. In a world of planned obsolescence, the mechanical watch is the last mass-produced object designed to outlive its owner. Buying one is a small rebellion against disposability — a vote for permanence in an impermanent age.

How to choose

The watch industry wants you confused. Complications, movements, manufacture versus ebauche, Geneva Seal versus COSC certification — the jargon exists partly to justify prices and partly because enthusiasts genuinely love the details. For the man buying his first serious watch, most of it is noise.

What matters comes down to three decisions. First, size. The trend has shifted decisively away from the oversized watches of the 2000s toward 36-40mm cases that fit under a cuff. If you can slide a finger between the watch and your wrist bone, it’s too big. Second, versatility. Your first serious watch should work with a suit and with jeans. That means a simple dial, no excessive complications, and a bracelet or strap in a neutral tone. Third, honesty. Buy the best watch you can afford from a maker with genuine horological credibility — not the fashion brand with a quartz movement and a luxury price tag.

The usual entry points remain the usual for good reason. A Seiko 5 Sports or Hamilton Khaki Field offers genuine mechanical horology under $1,000. The Longines HydroConquest or Oris Aquis delivers Swiss credibility around $2,000. The Omega Seamaster or Tudor Black Bay represents the sweet spot of luxury value at $4,000 to $6,000. Beyond that, you’re paying for finishing, heritage, and the ineffable satisfaction of owning something extraordinary.

The watch to avoid is the one bought for someone else’s approval. If you’re stretching to afford a Rolex because you think it will impress clients, you’ve already lost. The man who wears a $800 Hamilton with confidence out-dresses the man who wears a $15,000 Rolex with anxiety. The watch doesn’t make the gentleman. The gentleman makes the watch.

The counterargument: it’s just jewelry

The skeptics have a point, and it deserves a hearing. A mechanical watch is, functionally, obsolete. Your phone tells time more accurately. A $30 quartz watch keeps better time than a $30,000 mechanical one — quartz is simply more precise. Spending thousands on an inferior timekeeping device is, from a purely rational perspective, absurd.

The entire luxury watch industry rests on the proposition that rationality isn’t the point. And the market has validated that proposition so emphatically — record exports, waiting lists, auction records — that the skeptics have mostly gone quiet. But their underlying point lingers: at some level, this is about status, tribe, and self-image. The mechanical watch is jewelry that men are socially permitted to obsess over.

There’s truth in this. But it mistakes the nature of the objection. All clothing is signaling. All taste is tribal. The question isn’t whether a watch communicates status — of course it does. The question is whether it communicates something true. A well-chosen watch says: I value craftsmanship, I think long-term, I pay attention to details. Those are fine things to communicate. The man whose watch is chosen thoughtfully is already ahead of the man whose watch was chosen by a marketing department.

Why it matters more in 2026

The paradox of the digital age is that physical objects have gained meaning precisely because they’re unnecessary. Nobody needs a mechanical watch. Nobody needs fountain pens, film cameras, or vinyl records either — and all three have seen revivals among the same demographic buying watches. When the digital alternative is free and superior on every functional metric, choosing the analog version is a declaration of values.

For the modern gentleman, the watch is the anchor of a personal style philosophy. It’s the one object that’s always visible, always present, always saying something. In a world of fast fashion and faster upgrades, it’s the slowest thing you own — and that’s why it matters most.

Choose carefully. Wear daily. Service regularly. And when someone asks about it, you’ll have a story worth telling. That’s what the money buys — not timekeeping, but narrative.
