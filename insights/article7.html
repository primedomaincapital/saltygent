The Summer Jacket Problem: Linen, Seersucker, and the Fabric That Actually Breathes

SUMMER JACKETS · 2026 · 7 min read

It is ninety degrees, the wedding is outdoors, and the man in the linen blazer is the only person in the photograph who does not look like he is being punished. He is not cooler because linen is magic. He is cooler because the jacket was built for heat — the fabric, the weave, the lining, and the fit all working in the same direction — while the man next to him is wearing a suit that was built for an air-conditioned office and brought outside as an afterthought. The summer jacket problem is not finding a lighter jacket. It is understanding that a summer jacket is a different garment, not a thinner version of the winter one.

The first decision is the fabric, and it is the decision that matters most. Linen is the obvious answer and the complicated one. Woven from flax, it breathes exceptionally well, releases moisture quickly, and feels cool to the touch. It also wrinkles almost immediately — not slightly, not occasionally, but the moment you sit down. That is not a defect. It is the character of the cloth, and a man who cannot accept it should not buy linen. The wrinkles are what make a linen jacket look like expensive ease rather than a badly ironed uniform, and the difference between the two is the fit and the rest of the outfit, not the iron.

Seersucker is the fabric for men who want linen's airflow without linen's temperament. Its puckered surface holds the cloth slightly away from the skin, which lets air move between fabric and body, and the texture means it never looks wrinkled — it arrives wrinkled, by design. A modern seersucker in navy or olive, rather than the bright blue stripe of the Southern-lawyer cliché, is one of the most useful warm-weather jackets a man can own: light, fresh, and appropriate at a garden party, a country wedding, or a relaxed business lunch. The Flint and Tinder seersucker blazer is the pick the menswear writers keep returning to as the most breathable off the rack.

Tropical wool is the fabric for men who need to look formal in the heat, and it is the one most men overlook. A lightweight wool in an open weave — fresco, hopsack, or a tropical worsted — breathes, holds its shape, wrinkles less than linen, and looks significantly more formal. It sounds counterintuitive that wool could be cooler than cotton, but a dense cotton jacket traps heat while an open-weave wool lets it through, and the tailors who dress men for hot climates have known this for a century. The best summer sport coats from Proper Cloth use tropical wool from Drago, one of the most respected Italian mills, and the dressiest option on most lists is a linen-wool blend — Proper Cloth's 57 percent linen, 43 percent wool — which breathes like linen and holds its shape like wool.

The construction: what is inside the jacket

Fabric is half the story. Construction is the other half, and it is where most summer jackets fail. A summer blazer should be unstructured or soft-constructed: less internal fusing, less stiffness in the chest, a softer shoulder, a lighter fit. A fully lined jacket is warmer, especially if the lining is polyester — a plastic sheet that traps heat against the body. If the jacket has a full polyester lining, put it back on the rack. What you want is unlined, or half-lined, with a breathable lining in the sleeves only, so air passes through the fabric and reaches the back, where the ventilation is needed.

The weave matters as much as the fiber. Hopsack is not a fiber but a looser weave that leaves tiny gaps in the cloth, and a hopsack blazer in wool is one of the most useful summer jackets: neat, not overly formal, and good with chinos, linen trousers, jeans, and an open collar. Cotton-linen blends are the practical middle ground, combining some of linen's breathability with cotton's familiar softness and shape retention. Pure cotton can work, but it wrinkles more heavily than linen and does not recover its shape as well, which makes it the weakest of the summer fabrics for a jacket that needs to look pressed at dinner.

The price points are forgiving. A Tommy Hilfiger seersucker suit jacket runs about $140, and its linen suit jacket about $154 — the entry tier, and perfectly serviceable. The mid-tier, from makers like Relwen and Wills, runs to linen-cotton blends and unstructured cuts in the $200 to $400 range, with the Relwen Tropical Trap blazer built specifically for heat and the Wills Rosedale linen jacket cut with patch pockets and a partial canvas that conforms to the body over time. Made to measure from Proper Cloth sits above that, with the fit as the justification: a jacket cut to the body breathes better than a jacket that gaps at the chest, because the air circulates instead of pooling.

Color does quiet work in the heat. Navy is the first summer jacket for a reason: it is the most versatile color a man owns, it hides the sweat that linen cannot, and it pairs with white, blue, stone, and grey underneath without a second thought. Stone and natural linen come second, for the weekends and the coast, where the paler color reads as ease rather than effort. Olive and a muted green come third, for the man who already owns the navy and wants the jacket to do something different. The loud colors — the bright blues, the pinks, the patterns that shout — are the ones that date fastest, and a summer jacket is bought to be worn for years, not photographed once. Three colors in the rotation, and the jacket stops being a purchase and starts being a habit.

What goes under the jacket matters as much as the jacket itself. A poplin shirt in white or light blue is the default, and it is the right one: smooth, breathable, and formal enough for the jacket above it. A linen-cotton blend shirt is better still in real heat, because it breathes the way the jacket does and wrinkles the way the jacket does, so the two age together through the day instead of fighting each other. What does not work is the non-iron synthetic shirt, which traps heat against the skin and defeats the purpose of the breathable jacket above it. The rule is simple: if the shirt is plastic, the jacket is decoration. If the shirt breathes, the jacket works.

The counterargument: the jacket is optional

There is a case that the best summer jacket is no jacket at all, and in ninety-degree heat it is a strong one. A linen shirt, well cut, with the sleeves rolled and the collar open, is cooler than any jacket, and in most of America in July nobody expects a jacket. The jacket earns its place not by beating the shirt on temperature but by doing what the shirt cannot: shading the skin from direct sun, hiding the sweat, and carrying the outfit from a lunch into a dinner without a change of clothes. A good summer jacket, unlined and unstructured, shades the skin and keeps the wearer cooler than direct sunlight on a dark shirt — which is why the Italians, who dress for heat professionally, never gave it up.

Buy one jacket in navy, in a tropical wool or a linen-wool blend, unlined, with a soft shoulder. Buy a second in a natural linen or a navy seersucker, for the weekends and the weddings. Wear a poplin or linen-cotton shirt underneath — never a non-iron synthetic, which defeats the purpose of the breathable jacket above it. Steam the linen in the bathroom while the shower runs, and dry clean rarely, because the chemicals are harsh on natural fibers. The summer jacket problem has no perfect solution. It has a good one, and the good one is a jacket built for heat rather than a winter jacket that lost weight.
